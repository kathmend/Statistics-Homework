Modes of Convergence and Bayesian Inference

From the behavior of random sequences to learning from evidence: theory, simulations and statistical interpretation.

From sampling to inference

In HW4 we studied LLN and CLT; in HW5 sampling variability; in HW6 the normal CDF; and in HW7 independence and conditional frequencies. Here we formalize convergence and use conditional probability to update uncertainty.

01 · MODES OF CONVERGENCE

Four different meanings of approaching a limit

Convergence in probability

P(|Xₙ − X| > ε) → 0 for every ε > 0

The probability of a substantial error vanishes. This is the mode used by the weak LLN and consistency of estimators.

Almost sure convergence

P(lim Xₙ = X) = 1

Almost every entire sample path converges. This is the mode used by the strong LLN.

Mean-square convergence (L²)

E[(Xₙ − X)²] → 0

The average squared error vanishes. This is useful for studying estimators through their mean squared error.

Convergence in distribution

Fₙ(t) → F(t) at continuity points of F

The distributions approach a limiting distribution, not necessarily the individual random outcomes. This is the mode used by the CLT.

There is no general implication between almost sure and mean-square convergence.

Markov's inequality gives P(|Xₙ−X| > ε) ≤ E[(Xₙ−X)²]/ε². Almost sure convergence implies convergence in probability by bounded convergence of the error indicators. Convergence in distribution to a constant also implies convergence in probability.

Why the reverse implications fail

Example 1 — Small probability does not guarantee pathwise convergence. Let independent Xₙ equal 1 with probability 1/n and 0 otherwise. For 0<ε<1, the error probability is 1/n and E[Xₙ²]=1/n. Both vanish. But Σ1/n diverges, so by the second Borel–Cantelli lemma, ones occur infinitely often almost surely. Thus L² and probability convergence to zero do not imply almost sure convergence.

Example 2 — Rare but large errors. Let U be uniform on (0,1) and Xₙ=n·1{U≤1/n}. For almost every fixed U, eventually 1/n<U and Xₙ=0. Yet E[Xₙ²]=n²·(1/n)=n, which diverges. Almost sure convergence does not imply L² convergence.

Example 3 — Same distribution, different outcomes. Let X be equally likely to be −1 or +1 and Xₙ=−X. The CDFs are identical, but P(|Xₙ−X|>1)=1. Distribution convergence does not imply probability convergence.

02 · LLN AND CLT

Sample averages and their fluctuations

For i.i.d. observations with finite E|X|, the strong LLN gives X̄ₙ → μ almost surely, hence also in probability (weak LLN). With finite variance σ², E[(X̄ₙ−μ)²]=σ²/n, giving L² convergence too. The classical CLT, assuming i.i.d. observations and 0<σ²<∞, gives:

√n (X̄ₙ − μ)/σ → N(0,1) in distribution

The LLN describes where the mean goes; the CLT describes the distribution of its scaled fluctuations.

Why the Weak LLN holds with finite variance

For i.i.d. observations with mean μ and finite variance σ², E[X̄ₙ]=μ and Var(X̄ₙ)=σ²/n. Applying Chebyshev's inequality to X̄ₙ gives:

P(|X̄ₙ−μ| ≥ ε) ≤ σ²/(nε²) → 0 for every fixed ε > 0.

This proves convergence in probability under these assumptions. Finite variance is sufficient for this proof, but is not required by the general i.i.d. LLN with finite absolute mean.

03 · WHY √n?

Variance, covariance and independence

Var(ΣXᵢ) = ΣVar(Xᵢ) + 2Σᵢ<ⱼ Cov(Xᵢ,Xⱼ)

Independent finite-variance variables have zero covariance (uncorrelatedness alone is enough for variance additivity, not generally for the classical CLT). For i.i.d. observations, Var(ΣXᵢ)=nσ² and Var(X̄ₙ)=σ²/n. The standard error is σ/√n. Multiplying the centered mean by √n therefore preserves a nonzero variance. This scaling supports normal approximations and confidence intervals under suitable assumptions.

Experiment 3 — Correlated observations

Assume Xᵢ=μ+σ(√ρ Z₀+√(1−ρ)Zᵢ), with independent standard normal Z variables and 0≤ρ≤1. Every pair has correlation ρ.

Var(X̄ₙ)=σ²[1+(n−1)ρ]/n

Expanding the variance of the mean gives n diagonal variance terms σ² and n(n−1) off-diagonal covariance terms ρσ², divided by n². Thus the variance equals σ²/n + σ²ρ(n−1)/n. For example, with σ=1, n=100 and ρ=0.2, the independent variance is 0.01, but the correlated variance is 0.208. The effective sample size is n/[1+(n−1)ρ], approximately 4.81 here.

Positive common correlation prevents the variance from vanishing: the limit is σ²ρ. Ignoring dependence can make uncertainty intervals too narrow. Other dependence structures may still allow LLN or CLT under additional assumptions.

04 · CONDITIONAL PROBABILITY AND BAYES

Updating uncertainty with evidence

P(A|B)=P(A∩B)/P(B), P(B)>0

In probability this describes a known model; in statistics conditional probabilities may be estimated from observed frequencies, as in HW7. Since P(A∩B)=P(B|A)P(A), we obtain Bayes' theorem:

P(A|B)=P(B|A)P(A)/P(B)

For a parameter θ and data d, the prior π(θ) describes uncertainty before observing d, the likelihood L(θ;d)=p(d|θ) measures compatibility with the observed data, and the posterior π(θ|d) combines both. The likelihood is not generally a probability distribution over θ.

π(θ|d) = L(θ;d)π(θ) / ∫ L(u;d)π(u) du

For discrete parameters replace the integral by a sum. For example, choose a fair coin or a two-headed coin with equal prior probabilities. After observing one head, the posterior probability of the two-headed coin is (1×0.5)/(1×0.5+0.5×0.5)=2/3.

06 · DISCUSSION

Advantages, limitations and conclusions

Convergence

LLN supports consistent estimation; CLT supports approximate uncertainty quantification. Their assumptions matter: heavy tails or dependence may invalidate classical formulas. A finite simulation is not a proof.

Bayesian inference

Bayes combines prior knowledge and data, supports sequential learning and quantifies parameter uncertainty. Results depend on prior choice, likelihood quality, dependence and computational accuracy. Sensitivity analysis is essential.

Frequentist versus Bayesian inference

AspectFrequentistBayesian
ParameterFixed, unknownUncertainty represented by a distribution
EvidenceSampling model and observed dataLikelihood and prior
95% intervalProcedure covers the fixed parameter in 95% of repeated samples (exactly or approximately, depending on method)Contains 95% posterior probability conditional on data and model

Confidence interval versus credible interval

For independent normal observations with known σ, X̄ ± 1.96σ/√n is a 95% confidence interval (up to rounding). Its endpoints are random before sampling; μ is fixed. After observing the sample, the frequentist interpretation is not a 95% probability that μ lies inside this particular interval.

In Experiment 6, the equal-tailed credible interval uses the 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles of the Beta posterior. It directly expresses posterior uncertainty about θ. For the same binomial data, compare it with the approximate 95% Wilson confidence interval, which has repeated-sampling coverage close to 95% in many settings, not guaranteed exact coverage.

The two intervals answer different questions; neither is universally better. The Bayesian interval depends on the prior, while the Wilson interval does not.

Both approaches connect probability models to statistical conclusions. Independence simplifies variance and likelihood calculations, but must be justified rather than assumed automatically.
