Contingency Tables and Statistical Independence

Build a contingency table from paired observations, explore marginal and conditional frequencies, and test whether two categorical variables are associated.

01 · THE QUESTION

Are two variables independent?

Suppose we observe two categorical variables, X and F, for the same subjects. We first count how often each pair of categories occurs. Then we ask: does knowing X change the distribution of F?

Independence in probability
P(X = x, F = f) = P(X = x) · P(F = f)

For every pair of categories. Equivalently, P(F = f | X = x) = P(F = f) when P(X = x) > 0.

Statistical independence test
H₀: X and F are independent
H₁: X and F are associated

A sample can deviate from perfect independence just by chance. The chi-square test evaluates how unusual the observed deviations are under H₀.

07 · DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS

What can we conclude?

Probability vs. statistics

Probability: independence is a property of a joint probability distribution. Statistics: we only have a sample, so we compare observed counts with those expected if the population variables were independent.

Interpreting the p-value

A small p-value gives evidence against independence. A large p-value means we fail to reject independence; it does not prove independence. The significance level α is chosen before interpreting the test.

Assumptions and limitations

The observations should be independent, categories mutually exclusive, and expected counts sufficiently large for the chi-square approximation. For small expected counts, consider an exact test or collecting more observations.

Association is not causation

Even a strong association does not show that X causes F. Other factors, selection effects, or confounding variables may explain the relationship. Our cybersecurity datasets are illustrative, not causal experiments.
